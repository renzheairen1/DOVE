Our View of Self-Supervised Learning

Early research in artificial intelligence was heavily influenced by symbolic approaches. Researchers attempted to reduce intelligence to a set of explicitly representable symbols, rules, and logical operations. The world would first be encoded as concepts and propositions, after which answers would be derived through rule application, inference, and planning.

From this perspective, an intelligent system should resemble the highly rational machines portrayed in science fiction: it should be able to enumerate premises, apply rules, compare alternatives, and present a clear chain of reasoning.

Human intelligence, however, does not appear to operate primarily in this way.A large proportion of human perception, judgment, and action occurs rapidly and automatically, through processes that are difficult to translate into explicit rules. People do not necessarily have direct introspective access to the psychological processes that produce their judgments, and the verbal explanations they provide afterward may not faithfully reflect how the decisions were actually made. Intuition often arises before conscious reasoning, while reasoning sometimes serves only to explain, defend, or revise a judgment that has already been formed.

Consider a person choosing among dozens of brands of soy sauce in a supermarket. They are unlikely to compare every product’s ingredients, price, manufacturing process, and consumer reviews before calculating the theoretically optimal choice. Instead, they may decide quickly based on familiarity, packaging, or an overall impression. Only when asked to justify the decision might they say that one product has “better ingredients,” even though this consideration may not have played a meaningful role in the initial choice.

Jonathan Haidt famously described the relationship between intuition and reason using the metaphor of an elephant and its rider. The elephant represents rapid perception, emotional responses, associations, and behavioral tendencies shaped by accumulated experience. The rider represents explicit reasoning and planning. Although the rider appears to control the elephant, it is usually the elephant that supplies the direction and momentum. The rider can guide or correct it, but only under a few certain conditions.

We take this idea one step further and propose a hypothesis: reasoning may function partly as a compensation for insufficient intuition. When experience is limited, information is incomplete, or several intuitions conflict, a person cannot arrive at a reliable judgment directly. They must instead decompose the problem into smaller steps and reach an answer through a sequence of simpler intuitive judgments. In this sense, reasoning can be understood as the integration of many small intuitions.

As experience accumulates, however, a process that once required explicit reasoning may gradually be compressed into intuition. A beginner must follow rules and evaluate each component separately, whereas an expert can often recognize the critical features of a problem almost immediately. Through repeated observation, comparison, feedback, and analysis, experts reshape their intuitions until they can directly reach conclusions that previously required multiple stages of deliberate thought.

Reason, from this perspective, is less the foundation of intelligence than a form of scaffolding constructed while intuition is still immature. It handles problems that have not yet been sufficiently covered by experience. Once the structure of a problem has been internalized through extensive practice, the slow reasoning process can be transformed into fast and automatic judgment.

Powerful intuition is therefore not merely faster guessing. It is a mechanism that compresses a large body of past observations, feedback, and reasoning into judgments that can be called upon directly. When experience is sufficiently rich and internal representations are sufficiently accurate, intuition may point in the same direction as the conclusion produced by a long process of analysis. Reasoning may therefore not be the default operating mode of intelligence. It may instead be a compensatory mechanism activated when intuition cannot work reliably. As intelligence matures, more problems that once required deliberate reasoning can potentially be converted into intuition.

Deep Learning as Compressed Experience

The rise of deep learning changed the source of capability in artificial intelligence.

Neural networks no longer require researchers to specify complete sets of rules for recognizing faces, understanding language, or identifying objects. Instead, they adjust their internal parameters through exposure to large numbers of examples. Regularities in experience are compressed into the model, allowing tasks that appear to require complex analysis to be performed at inference time through a rapid forward pass.

The resulting representations and decision mechanisms may be difficult to interpret fully, but they can operate quickly and effectively. As a functional analogy, this is closer to experience-shaped intuition than to explicit reasoning carried out rule by rule.

Deep learning, however, does not automatically eliminate human priors.

Although supervised learning removes the need to manually encode every inference rule, it still depends heavily on humans to define the task and provide the labels. Researchers first decide which categories the model should distinguish or which outcomes it should predict. Human annotations are then used to tell the model the correct answer for each example.

This approach is explicit and efficient, but every label is necessarily a compression of reality. When a complex image is labeled simply as “gingivitis,” the label preserves only a small fraction of the information contained in the image. It ignores morphology, spatial relationships, environmental context, variations in disease state, and many other forms of potentially meaningful structure. The model is therefore encouraged to accumulate experience around a narrowly defined objective. It may learn the information required to reproduce the label while discarding structures that are irrelevant to the current task but potentially valuable for future ones.

Self-Supervised Learning as the Formation of Broad Intuition

Self-supervised learning attempts to bypass this bottleneck.Rather than depending on humans to assign a target category to every example, it constructs learning signals from the data itself. A model may predict masked words or image regions from their context, predict the future from the past, learn representations that remain stable across different views of the same object, or predict the latent representations produced by another network. The raw data serve both as the input and as the source of supervision.

The significance of self-supervised learning therefore extends beyond reducing annotation costs.More importantly, it allows models to learn from an amount of experience far greater than manual annotation could realistically provide. Before a specific downstream task has even been defined, a model can begin to capture recurring structures, relationships, and statistical regularities in the data. Its representations do not have to be organized solely around category boundaries selected in advance by humans. They may preserve a richer and more transferable set of latent information, compressing substantial aspects of the world’s structure into the model.

In this sense, self-supervised learning attempts to form a broad foundational intuition before the specific question is known.

When a new task later appears, the model does not have to learn entirely from scratch. It may require only a relatively small adaptation of its existing experiential structure. This has a functional similarity to human development: long before receiving formal education or learning professional concepts, people have already acquired a large amount of implicit knowledge through sustained perception and interaction with the world.

The history of artificial intelligence can therefore be viewed, at least in part, as a gradual shift toward mechanisms that resemble intuition and away from systems whose behavior is determined directly by human-specified rules and categories.

Self-Supervision Does Not Eliminate Priors

Self-supervised learning reduces certain forms of human prior, but it does not eliminate them.

Different self-supervised objectives encourage models to preserve different aspects of the data. Masked autoencoders, or MAEs, favor information useful for reconstructing missing pixels. DINO emphasizes semantic consistency across different views of the same image. iBOT requires models to predict latent representations for masked image regions.

The model is not discovering the world from a completely neutral position. It is discovering patterns within a learning problem designed by researchers.

Current self-supervised systems also remain largely passive learners of fixed datasets. A model typically cannot decide what it should observe next, which action it should take, or which experiment it should conduct. It may learn correlations from existing images and videos, but it cannot easily distinguish causal relationships by intervening in the environment itself.

Even an extremely large collection of passive internet data is not equivalent to the experience of a learner that continuously interacts with the real world.

Fixed training objectives impose another limitation. Existing models are generally optimized according to loss functions specified in advance by researchers. Even when feedback is delayed, researchers still largely determine what counts as success and which errors should be reduced.

Human learning is different in an important respect. People do not pursue only immediate and clearly defined outcomes. They can organize their actions around vague, long-term, and uncertain goals. They can also reinterpret or modify their goals as new experience accumulates. A person may continue learning for an imagined future even when no immediate signal can determine whether each individual step is correct.

Future research may therefore need to move beyond the question of how to optimize a fixed objective. It may also need to address how an intelligent system can decide what is worth learning when its goals are ambiguous, and how those goals should be revised as its experience changes.
